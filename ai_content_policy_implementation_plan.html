Phase 1 · Weeks 1–2
Assess and justify
Build the case before you write a single line of policy
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
1 Audit current AI tool usage Survey your five writers informally. List every AI tool in active use — Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, etc. Note what each is used for: drafting, editing, code samples, translations. This becomes your policy scope. You Tool inventory
2 Identify risks specific to your context Map risks against your actual use cases. Key ones: confidential trading logic in prompts, hallucinated API specs published as truth, vendor LLMs training on proprietary inputs, unreviewed AI output in client-facing docs. You Risk list
3 Check for existing firm policies Ask legal, compliance, or IT security whether an AI use policy, data classification policy, or acceptable use policy already exists. Your policy must align with or extend these — not contradict them. This also signals you are governance-aware. Cross-functional Alignment notes
4 Write a one-paragraph justification Not a formal document — a clear internal statement of why this policy is needed now. Cover: team already uses AI tools, no current standards exist, risk of errors in ML model docs, reputational and compliance exposure. This becomes the policy preamble. You Policy preamble
Phase 2 · Weeks 3–4
Draft the policy
Write the actual one-page document
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
5 Define the five core sections Keep it to one page. Sections: (1) Purpose — why the policy exists. (2) Scope — who and what it covers. (3) Approved tools — what is permitted and under what conditions. (4) Review requirements — what AI-assisted content must be reviewed before publishing. (5) Prohibited uses — what is never allowed, e.g. inputting confidential data. You Policy outline
6 Draft the prohibited uses clause This is the highest-stakes section. Specify: no confidential trading strategies, model parameters, or client data in external AI prompts. No publishing AI-generated content without human review. No use of unapproved tools. Write this in plain language, not legal language. You Draft clause
7 Define the review standard State explicitly what "reviewed" means — not just read, but verified for technical accuracy against source material. Specify who is accountable: the writer who used the AI tool is responsible for the accuracy of the output. This creates a clear accountability chain. You Review standard
8 Decide on disclosure requirements Determine whether internal documents need to note AI was used in drafting. For internal docs and model cards, a metadata field or footer note is reasonable. For external or client-facing content, the bar is higher. Make a clear call and write it into the policy. You Disclosure rule
Phase 3 · Weeks 5–6
Present and get sign-off
Turn a document into an adopted policy
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
9 Present to your manager first Do not send the policy cold — walk your manager through it in a meeting. Frame it as: the team is already using AI tools, this formalizes safe practice, it protects the firm. Ask for their endorsement before wider circulation. Their support is what makes it a policy rather than a suggestion. Cross-functional Manager endorsement
10 Route to legal or compliance for review Even a one-page internal policy benefits from a legal or compliance review in a hedge fund context. It signals seriousness and ensures your prohibited uses clause aligns with the firm's data security obligations. Ask for a lightweight review, not a full legal opinion. Cross-functional Legal review note
11 Present to your team and explain why Call a team meeting. Do not simply circulate the document — explain the reasoning. Cover: what risks this addresses, what changes in day-to-day workflow, what it does not restrict. Invite questions. The goal is understanding and buy-in, not compliance by decree. Writers who understand the why will self-enforce. You Team acknowledgment
12 Get written acknowledgment Ask each team member to confirm receipt and understanding — an email reply is sufficient. Keep this. It is your implementation evidence. A policy that was distributed but never acknowledged is not an implemented policy from a governance standpoint. Evidence Sign-off emails
Phase 4 · Ongoing
Implement and maintain
Make the policy live — and build the audit trail
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
13 Build a review checklist Create a short checklist writers complete before publishing AI-assisted content: tool used, content type, data classification check, accuracy verified against source, reviewer name. Can be a simple form in Confluence or a doc template. This operationalizes the policy into a daily workflow artifact. You Review checklist
14 Track compliance informally In your regular 1:1s, ask each writer what AI tools they used that week and whether the review checklist was followed. You are not auditing — you are reinforcing. Note any patterns where the policy is unclear or impractical and use those to refine it. This shows the policy is living, not filed and forgotten. You 1:1 notes
15 Schedule a quarterly review AI tools change fast. Set a calendar reminder to review the policy every quarter. Check whether new tools have entered use, whether the prohibited uses list is still appropriate, whether the review standard needs tightening. Version the document and keep prior versions. This is what makes it a maintained governance artifact. Evidence Version history
16 Document what you implemented After 90 days, write a one-paragraph internal summary: what the policy covers, when it was adopted, who signed off, what changed in team practice as a result. This is the artifact that lets you say "I implemented" rather than "I drafted." Keep it with your performance review notes and your governance portfolio. Evidence Impact summary
