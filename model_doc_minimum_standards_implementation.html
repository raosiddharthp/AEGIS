Phase 1 · Weeks 1–2
Audit the current state
Understand what documentation exists before defining what should
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
1 Inventory all ML models in production Work with engineering or model risk to list every ML model currently running. For each, note: does documentation exist, where does it live, who owns it, when was it last updated. Even a rough spreadsheet reveals the gap immediately. This is your baseline — the before state you will later compare against. Cross-functional Model inventory
2 Assess documentation quality across models For each model with existing documentation, evaluate what is present and what is missing. Common gaps: no stated intended use, no data source description, no limitations section, no owner or review date, no risk classification. Score each model informally — complete, partial, or absent. This gap analysis is itself a governance deliverable. You Gap analysis
3 Read SR 11-7 and NIST AI RMF for field requirements SR 11-7 is the Federal Reserve's model risk management guidance — directly applicable to your hedge fund context. NIST AI RMF covers broader AI documentation requirements. Both are free and publicly available. Read specifically for what documentation each framework says must exist. Extract the fields they require — these become your minimum standard fields. Do this before drafting anything. You Required fields list
4 Interview model owners and engineers Schedule 30-minute conversations with the engineers or quants who own each model. Ask three questions: what does this model do, what would someone need to know to use it safely, and what would an auditor ask about it that you could not currently answer from existing documentation. Their answers define what the standard needs to capture. Cross-functional Interview notes
Phase 2 · Weeks 3–4
Define the standard
Specify exactly what every model document must contain
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
5 Define the mandatory fields Based on your SR 11-7 reading and interviews, define the non-negotiable fields every model document must contain regardless of model complexity. Recommended minimum: Model name Owner Intended use Data inputs Known limitations Risk classification Human oversight requirement Review date Approval sign-off. Keep it to seven to nine fields. More than that and engineers will not complete it. You Field definitions
6 Define the risk classification tiers Create a simple three-tier classification: low risk (internal tooling, no decision impact), medium risk (informs but does not drive decisions), high risk (outputs directly influence trading or client outcomes). Each tier determines the documentation depth required and the review frequency. In your hedge fund context, anything touching the trading floor defaults to high risk. Cross-functional Risk tiers
7 Build the intake form Translate the mandatory fields into a structured intake form that model owners complete before documentation begins. This is the enforcement mechanism — documentation cannot start without it. Keep it short enough to complete in 20 minutes. Use a Confluence template, a Google Form, or a structured doc — whatever your firm already uses. The format matters less than the adoption. You Intake form
8 Write the one-page standards document The policy document itself states: purpose, scope, mandatory fields by risk tier, the intake process, the review and approval workflow, and the consequence of non-compliance — meaning no model proceeds to production documentation without the intake form completed. Write it in plain language. This is the governance artifact you will get signed off. You Standards document
Phase 3 · Weeks 5–6
Validate with a pilot model
Test the standard against a real model before firm-wide rollout
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
9 Select a pilot model Pick one model from your inventory for the pilot run. Choose one that is medium complexity — not the simplest model, which proves nothing, and not the most complex trading model, which creates too much friction. Ideally pick one whose engineer is cooperative and whose documentation is currently partial. That gives you a realistic test of whether the standard is completable. Cross-functional Pilot model selected
10 Run the intake form with the model owner Sit with the model owner and complete the intake form together for the pilot model. Do not send it cold — do it collaboratively the first time. Note where they hesitate, where fields are unclear, where information does not exist. Every friction point is a signal to refine the form before wider rollout. Budget 45 minutes for this session. Engineering Completed intake
11 Produce the pilot model card Using the completed intake form, produce the full model card document for the pilot model. This is the first document produced under the new standard. It demonstrates that the standard is workable, produces a useful output, and gives you something concrete to show stakeholders. It is also your first implementation evidence — a real model, documented to a defined standard, with a traceable intake process. You Pilot model card
12 Gather feedback and refine the standard After the pilot, ask the model owner and any reviewers: what was unclear, what was missing, what was unnecessary. Incorporate feedback into a revised version of the intake form and standards document. Version both documents — v0.1 to v1.0. The revision itself demonstrates the standard is maintained and responsive, not static. You Revised v1.0 standard
Phase 4 · Weeks 7–10
Get sign-off and roll out
Make it official and apply it across the model inventory
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
13 Present to model risk and technology leadership Present the v1.0 standard, the pilot model card, and the gap analysis to your manager and whoever owns model risk — likely the CRO or head of technology risk. Show the before state from your gap analysis and the after state from the pilot model card. Frame it as closing a regulatory exposure. Ask for formal endorsement. Their sign-off is what gives the standard authority beyond your team. Cross-functional Leadership sign-off
14 Communicate the standard to engineering teams Send a clear, brief communication to model owners and engineers: what the standard requires, how the intake process works, where the form lives, and your team's role in the process. Emphasize that the intake form is a collaboration, not an audit. Offer to walk any model owner through their first intake session. Reduce friction at rollout or adoption will stall. You Rollout comms
15 Prioritize backfill by risk tier Apply the standard retroactively to existing models in priority order: high-risk models first, then medium, then low. Do not try to document everything simultaneously — that creates bottlenecks and partial completions, which are worse than a clear queue. Set a realistic timeline: two to three high-risk models per month. Track progress against your model inventory spreadsheet. You Backfill schedule
16 Establish the production gate Work with engineering and model risk to establish a formal expectation: no new model enters production without a completed intake form and a model card at minimum standard. This does not need to be a hard technical block immediately — a process agreement is sufficient to start. Document the agreement in writing. This is the control that makes the standard a governance mechanism rather than a recommendation. Cross-functional Gate agreement
Phase 5 · Ongoing
Maintain and build the audit trail
Keep the standard alive and make implementation citable
#TaskWhat it entailsWhoOutput
17 Maintain the model documentation register Keep your inventory spreadsheet current. For each model: document status, risk tier, intake completion date, model card version, last review date, owner. Review it monthly. This register is the single source of truth for your firm's model documentation coverage — and it is the first thing an auditor or regulator would ask to see. You Living register
18 Schedule periodic document reviews Set review triggers for each model card: annual review at minimum, plus re-review whenever the model is retrained, its inputs change, or its use case expands. Assign review ownership to the model owner, not your team — your team maintains the document, but the model owner validates accuracy. Track review dates in your register. Engineering Review schedule
19 Report coverage metrics to leadership Quarterly, produce a one-paragraph summary for your manager: how many models are documented to standard, how many are in progress, how many are outstanding, and whether any new models entered production since the last report. This makes documentation coverage visible as a governance metric — and positions you as the person who owns and tracks it. Evidence Coverage report
20 Write your implementation summary After six months, write a concise summary of what you built: the standard, the intake process, the number of models documented, the gap it closed, who signed off. Include version history of the standard document and a sample model card with identifying details redacted. This is your governance portfolio artifact — the evidence behind the claim that you designed and implemented a model documentation framework at a regulated financial firm. Evidence Portfolio artifact
